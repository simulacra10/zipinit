Political power becomes more dangerous as it becomes more concentrated.

When fewer representatives speak for larger populations, each office carries more power, more communities must compete for the same attention, and the distance between citizens and their representatives grows. What appears efficient from the perspective of administration may be deeply inefficient from the perspective of representation.

Representative government exists to connect the people to political power through officials who are chosen by them and accountable to them. Its purpose is not to eliminate disagreement or reduce the number of voices involved in public decisions.

That requires power to be divided.

Decentralization creates more points of representation, more paths for citizens to reach their government, and more obstacles for any single institution, party, or interest to control the whole. It may produce more debate and require more negotiation, but those are not defects in a representative system. They are signs that political authority has not been compressed into too few hands.

We already depend on decentralized systems every day.

The Internet is decentralized. No single router controls the movement of all information. Data travels through many networks and can often be redirected when one path fails. The system is resilient because it does not depend on one central route or one universal gatekeeper.

Bitcoin is decentralized. No single bank, company, or government maintains the entire ledger. Verification is distributed across a network of participants. Whatever one thinks of Bitcoin as money or as an investment, its underlying structure demonstrates an important principle: a system can maintain a reliable shared record without placing total control in one institution.

Open-source software operates in a similar way. Linux is built and maintained by developers, companies, foundations, and volunteers around the world. There are maintainers and leaders, but no single company owns the entire ecosystem. If one organization abandons a project, others can continue it, modify it, or build from it.

Markets also depend on decentralization. Millions of buyers, sellers, workers, and producers make decisions based on local knowledge. No central planner knows every shortage, preference, price, skill, or production constraint. Prices coordinate this dispersed knowledge imperfectly, but more effectively than any single institution could hope to do.

Even the English language is decentralized.

There is no single authority with final power to determine what English is. Dictionaries document usage, but they do not command it. Words emerge from neighborhoods, industries, professions, regions, immigrant communities, music, technology, and ordinary conversation. Meanings change because people change how they speak.

English remains understandable because it has shared rules and conventions. It remains adaptable because no central authority controls every word. Its structure is maintained through common use rather than comprehensive command.

These examples differ in important ways, but they share one advantage: they distribute knowledge and authority.

Representation should be understood through the same principle.

## Centralization Creates Distance

Political consolidation creates distance between citizens and the people exercising power on their behalf.

When one representative speaks for a large population spread across a broad district, distinct communities are treated as a single political unit. Local concerns become aggregated into averages. Smaller communities compete for attention against larger population centers. Residents who share a representative may have little else in common.

The representative may still be responsive, conscientious, and hardworking. The structural problem remains. One person can maintain only so many meaningful relationships with so many communities.

Delegate Thomas Hutchinson represents District 37B, which includes portions of Caroline, Dorchester, Talbot, and Wicomico counties. Those counties share some regional concerns, but their economies, institutions, population centers, and local priorities differ. At times, those interests conflict.

One representative must therefore divide his attention among communities spread across four counties, each competing to make its concerns visible. The problem is not the character or effort of the representative. The problem is the structure of the district.

A more decentralized system would assign representation to smaller, more coherent geographic communities. More communities would have a direct voice, fewer local concerns would be submerged within a large multicounty district, and political authority would be distributed among more representatives rather than concentrated in a single office.

Centralization also increases the value of controlling the system from above.

When a legislature contains relatively few seats, each seat carries more power. Political parties, donors, interest groups, and statewide organizations can concentrate their resources on a smaller number of officials. Control over district boundaries becomes more consequential because every boundary determines which communities will be grouped together and which voters will be separated.

A centralized representational system therefore creates fewer points of access and fewer points of resistance.

That is not efficiency. It is concentrated power.

Decentralization creates more offices, more constituencies, and more independent relationships through which the people can reach their government. It also makes political control harder to consolidate because influence must be exercised across a larger number of representatives who answer to distinct communities.

That is not inefficiency. It is a safeguard.

## More Representation Means Less Concentrated Power

The purpose of representation is not simply to produce legislation. It is to preserve a clear relationship between a community and the person authorized to act on its behalf.

A representative should know whom he represents. The public should know who is responsible. A constituency should be stable enough for relationships to develop over time. Accountability should not require citizens to understand a district assembled from fragments of multiple counties, towns, and neighborhoods.

The larger and more artificial a district becomes, the weaker that relationship becomes.

ZIP Apportionment addresses this problem by beginning with existing geographic communities rather than drawing population-based districts through a centralized political process. ZIP codes are familiar, relatively stable, and tied to places people already recognize.

Under ZIP Apportionment, representation in Maryland would be distributed across more locally rooted constituencies. Larger ZIP codes could receive additional representation or be divided according to clear, predetermined rules. Very small ZIP codes could be joined with adjacent communities. Maryland would determine how to handle these limited exceptions, but the governing principle would remain: representation begins with place.

This would decentralize political power in several ways.

First, it would increase the number of meaningful points of representation. More communities would have their own direct voice instead of being submerged within larger districts.

Second, it would reduce dependence on centralized mapmaking. Representation would no longer begin with political actors drawing boundaries around selected populations.

Third, it would make representatives more accessible. A smaller constituency gives residents a greater opportunity to know their representative, be heard, and organize effectively.

Fourth, it would make political capture more difficult. Influencing a handful of powerful representatives is easier than influencing a much larger body of officials accountable to distinct communities.

Fifth, it would preserve local knowledge. Communities understand their own industries, institutions, and cultural relationships better than distant officials or statewide planners.

## Decentralization Preserves Knowledge

One of the strongest arguments for decentralization is that knowledge is dispersed.

No central authority possesses all the information needed to govern every community well. State officials may understand statewide policy, but they cannot fully understand every neighborhood, town, business district, agricultural region, school system, transportation problem, or local institution.

The people who live in a place possess knowledge that cannot be reduced to census tables or demographic categories.

They know which roads flood.

They know which schools are struggling.

They know which businesses are closing.

They know which employers hold a town together.

They know which local traditions, churches, civic organizations, volunteer companies, and public spaces give the community its identity.

When representation is centralized into large districts, much of that knowledge is filtered out. A representative must prioritize the loudest, largest, or most politically valuable concerns.

Decentralized representation gives local knowledge a formal place in government. It does not guarantee that every representative will use that knowledge wisely. It does guarantee that more communities will have someone responsible for carrying that knowledge into the legislative process.

## Decentralization Allows Experimentation

Centralized systems tend to make one decision for everyone.

Decentralized systems allow communities to try different approaches. One locality may solve a problem in a way that others can observe and adopt. Another may fail without imposing that failure on the entire state.

This is one of the traditional strengths of federalism. States and municipalities can test policies, compare results, and adapt. The same principle applies to representation.

A legislature composed of more locally grounded representatives would contain a wider range of experience. Rural communities, small towns, suburbs, cities, industrial areas, and coastal regions would not merely appear as demographic categories within a few large districts. They would speak through representatives directly connected to those places.

More representation does not guarantee agreement. It guarantees that disagreement is visible.

That, frankly, is healthy.

A legislature should not conceal political conflict by compressing many communities into a small number of districts. It should provide an orderly institution in which those differences can be expressed, negotiated, and resolved.

## Decentralization Requires Structure

None of this means that every power should be pushed to the smallest possible unit.

Some problems require coordination across communities. Transportation networks, environmental systems, criminal law, interstate commerce, public health, and national defense cross local boundaries. A functioning government needs common constitutional rules and institutions capable of addressing genuinely shared problems.

Decentralization also does not mean that local officials should be free from oversight. Local power can be abused just as state or federal power can be abused.

The answer is not fragmentation. It is divided authority, clear responsibility, and constitutional limits.

The Internet still uses common protocols.

English still has grammar and shared conventions.

Open-source projects still have maintainers.

Markets still require rules against fraud and theft.

Federalism still operates under a constitution.

Decentralized representation should work the same way. Representatives would operate within a common legal framework, but political voice would be distributed among more stable, locally rooted constituencies.

## The People Benefit When Power Is Divided

Centralized government is often defended in the name of efficiency. But government is not a delivery company, representation is not a production line, and citizens are not units to be processed through an administrative system.

The goal is not to govern the largest possible population through the fewest possible decision-makers.

The goal is to preserve self-government.

That requires enough representatives for communities to remain visible. It requires constituencies that ordinary people can recognize and understand. It requires political authority to be divided so that no small group can easily control the whole.

More representation means more points of access.

More points of access mean more opportunities to be heard.

More representatives mean political power is divided among more people.

More locally rooted constituencies mean political knowledge remains connected to place.

ZIP Apportionment is therefore not merely a different method of assigning legislative seats. It is a decentralization of representation itself.

It moves political authority away from centralized mapmaking and toward stable communities. It increases the number of direct relationships between citizens and representatives. It makes Maryland less dependent on a few powerful districts and the institutions positioned to control them.

Decentralization can look untidy. It can produce disagreement. It can slow decisions.

Those are not always defects.

Sometimes friction prevents power from moving too quickly.

Sometimes redundancy keeps a system from failing.

Sometimes inefficiency is the price of accountability.

And sometimes the best way to protect the people from concentrated political power is to give them more representation, closer to home.
