Apportionment by population, as we practice it now, reminds me of dodgeball or baseball back in elementary school.

The coach would pick two kids he already knew were good at sports and make them team captains. Then each captain would take turns choosing players.

Everybody knew who the good players were. So the real advantage came from choosing first. If you won the coin toss, you got the cream of the crop. Then the captains worked their way down the line until they got to the last handful of misfits — kids like me — who were terrible at sports.

At that point, it was not about who was good. It was about who was least bad.

By the time the last player was picked, everyone already knew which team had the advantage. The game had not even started, but the outcome had already been shaped by the selection process.

That is what our current system of political districting often feels like.

The voters are lined up, the captains are chosen, and then Team A and Team B take turns trying to build the most favorable map. One side gets to draw the lines, or influence the process, and those lines become power. They decide which voters are grouped together, which communities are divided, which voices are amplified, and which ones are made easier to ignore.

If your neighborhood, town, or community happens to fit neatly inside the winning team’s strategy, then the system may appear to work for you. But if you are one of the voters who gets packed, cracked, split, or swallowed up for someone else’s advantage, then your representation is weakened before the election ever begins.

That is the problem.

The game is not only decided by who votes. It is often decided by who gets to choose the teams.
